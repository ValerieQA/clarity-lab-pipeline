# Clarity Lab — Scene Bank

This file is appended to every content prompt. Edit it by hand; no code changes needed.

A scene is one person, one moment, one concrete detail. It is the opening of a Clarity Lab text.

The scene exists for recognition before explanation. The reader should be able to think, *I know that exact moment*, before the text tells her what it may mean.

---

## Rules for using a scene

Pick one, or write a new one in the same register.

**Do not reuse a scene within 40 published pieces.** Check the recent-scenes list passed with the topic.

**One person, one moment.** Do not begin with a category of people or a general condition.

**Describe the action before the interpretation.** Write *she closes the laptop and the room is quiet again*, not *she feels alone*.

**Concrete before abstract.** A browser tab, a dinner table, an unfinished voice note, a phone call, a sentence in an old notebook.

**No names.** She, he, someone, or the person. Never invent a name merely to make the scene appear specific.

**No invented biography.** Do not add a profession, marriage, children, diagnosis, nationality, or other personal fact unless the topic brief requires it.

**Do not resolve the scene.** Its job is to open recognition, not deliver the lesson.

**Change the surface when needed.** A structural moment may happen in a text message, call, notebook, browser tab, dinner, session, or conversation. Keep the human structure while varying the physical details.

The Scene Bank is a source, not a mandatory script. New scenes are encouraged when the topic needs one.

# The bank

## TOO MANY MAPS

Scenes about having accumulated explanations without arriving at one whole picture.

**S01 — The four explanations.** Four browser tabs are open. Each explains the same behaviour differently. She can see herself in all four.

**S02 — The old notebook.** She opens notes from three years ago. The terminology is different. The question underneath it is the same.

**S03 — The saved results.** A folder on her phone contains screenshots from tests, readings, assessments, and charts. She has not opened it in months. She has not deleted it either.

**S04 — The good therapist.** She closes the laptop after a session and writes down something genuinely useful. There are already six notebooks like this on the shelf.

**S05 — The fourth course.** She watches the first lesson and recognises the framework before the instructor finishes explaining it.

**S06 — The search after the answer.** She gets an explanation that makes sense. Twenty minutes later she is searching the same question with different words.

**S07 — The vocabulary.** She can explain exactly why she does it. The explanation takes less than a minute now.

**S08 — The conflicting maps.** One assessment says she needs more structure. Another says structure is the problem. Both descriptions sound accurate.

**S09 — Monday morning.** The book is finished, the notes are highlighted, and Monday morning still requires an actual decision.

**S10 — The new system.** She reaches the section describing her type and knows the paragraph will feel accurate before she reads it.

---

## ALIEN

Scenes about living slightly outside the default script without turning that experience into an identity claim.

**S11 — The dinner table.** Everyone is talking about mortgages and school districts. She is following the conversation and wondering when everyone learned to want the same things.

**S12 — Two feet behind.** She is answering someone normally and suddenly notices herself answering, as though the conversation is happening a small distance in front of her.

**S13 — The body.** She has been working for hours. Standing up is the first moment she notices she is hungry.

**S14 — The correct reaction.** Someone tells her good news. She knows the appropriate response immediately and hears herself give it.

**S15 — The group photo.** Everyone moves together for the picture. She watches for half a second to see where people put their hands before doing the same.

**S16 — The instructions nobody gave.** A conversation changes tone and everyone else appears to know why.

**S17 — The normal plan.** Someone describes the next five years as though the sequence is obvious. She waits for the part that explains why those are the next five things.

**S18 — The empty room after the call.** The online conversation ends. She closes the laptop and the people who understood exactly what she meant are suddenly nowhere in the room.

**S19 — The translation.** She starts answering a simple question, stops, and replaces the first answer with the version that will require less explanation.

**S20 — The default person.** A form asks a question with three obvious options. She reads them twice because none is quite true.

---

## NO ONE OLDER

Scenes about having questions without an obvious person who can hold them.

**S21 — The call home.** She calls her mother with one question and realises halfway through the answer that they are talking about two different worlds.

**S22 — The unsent message.** She types a long message to a friend, reads the previous conversation, and deletes it.

**S23 — The fourth telling.** She starts telling the same person what happened and hears herself say, “I know we've talked about this already.”

**S24 — The voice note.** Eleven minutes recorded. She listens to the first few seconds and deletes it without sending.

**S25 — The capable one.** Someone calls her because they need to think something through. She stays on the phone until they know what they are going to do. Afterwards she opens her own notes.

**S26 — The old answer.** She asks someone older what they would do. The answer begins with how things worked twenty years ago.

**S27 — The search box.** She types a question into Google that she would rather have asked a person.

**S28 — The therapist boundary.** Something happens on Thursday. Her next session is Tuesday. She opens the notes app instead.

**S29 — The partner pause.** She starts the sentence and sees recognition on her partner's face before she reaches the end. They have had this conversation before.

**S30 — The almost-call.** Her thumb stops over one contact, then another. The phone goes back on the table.

## CLARITY LAB SHOWN

Scenes for content that demonstrates what Clarity Lab actually does.

Use these only when the topic brief provides enough real product material to support the demonstration. Never invent a user, reading, result, or testimonial.

**S31 — The repeated sentence.** The same phrase appears in three answers written weeks apart. It was not marked as important in any of them.

**S32 — The missing subject.** Several answers describe what needs to happen. Almost none say who is going to do it.

**S33 — The two answers together.** Each answer makes sense on its own. Placed next to each other, they point in opposite directions.

**S34 — The unchanged wording.** An answer from months ago and an answer from today use almost exactly the same sentence.

**S35 — The available options.** She describes the situation at length and names two possible choices. A third possibility is visible elsewhere in her own answers.

**S36 — The explanation that repeats.** Different questions produce the same explanation.

**S37 — The first reading.** Nothing new has been added to her story. The difference is that several pieces of it are visible at the same time.

**S38 — The language on the page.** A sentence looks ordinary until it is placed beside five other sentences written in the same way.

## CROSS-TERRITORY SCENES

These can support more than one strategic territory.

**S39 — The smooth answer.** Someone asks how things are going. Her answer comes out complete enough that there is no obvious second question.

**S40 — The reread notebook.** She opens a notebook from a year ago and finds the same question written in different vocabulary.

**S41 — The research tab.** Nineteen tabs are open around a question she has already answered several times.

**S42 — The cancelled session.** She cancels because she already knows exactly what she will say when she gets there.

**S43 — The advice she gives.** Someone describes a problem. Her answer for them is immediate. Later she returns to her own version of the same problem.

**S44 — The unsent draft.** She rewrites the message four times and leaves it in drafts.

## Adding a new scene

Format:

**Sxx — Short label.** One or two sentences. One person, one moment, concrete action.

Before adding it, test it:

1. **Could this describe almost anyone?**
   Then it is too wide. Make the moment more specific.

2. **Does it tell the reader what she feels?**
   Replace the feeling with something she does, says, deletes, opens, closes, rereads, checks, or leaves unfinished.

3. **Does it explain its own meaning?**
   Remove the explanation. The article will do that work.

4. **Did you invent biography to make it specific?**
   Remove the invented detail unless the topic requires it.

5. **Does it sound like a quote for Instagram?**
   It is probably an aphorism, not a scene.

6. **Does it belong to the current Clarity Lab strategy?**
   It should connect naturally to Too Many Maps, Alien, No One Older, or Clarity Lab Shown.

Specificity comes from the moment, not from adding fictional detail.
